## General instructions

The assignment is completed in groups of two to three students. Members are self-selected. The group selects one paper from the list below and confirms the choice by e-mail no later than **September 15, 5 pm**. No more than two groups may work on the same paper, allocated first-come, first-served. Groups that have not selected a paper by the deadline are assigned both a group and a paper.

Each group must complete **three tasks**, weighted equally.

### Task 1 — One-page policy summary

Write a one-page summary of the paper aimed at an informed but non-specialist audience, following the style of the *IZA World of Labor*. An example is available on the course page.

The summary should explain:

- the research question,
- the empirical strategy,
- the main findings,
- the policy relevance,

without reproducing the abstract or other parts of the paper.

### Task 2 — Lithuanian application (500–1,000 words)

Discuss whether and how the findings of the paper are relevant for Lithuania. The argument must be supported by data, institutional evidence, and/or relevant research. Simply stating that Lithuania is "similar" or "different" from the country studied in the paper is not sufficient.

### Task 3 — Professional presentation and discussion

Prepare a **10-minute presentation** explaining:

- the research question,
- the empirical strategy,
- the main result,
- the implications for Lithuania.

Rules:

- notes are **not allowed** during the presentation;
- all group members must introduce themselves at the beginning and participate meaningfully;
- the presentation is followed by questions from the class and from the lecturer;
- all group members must understand and defend the **entire** assignment, not only the part they personally prepared.

Individual performance in presentations and participation in discussions may lead to differences in grades within the same group.

## Academic and professional expectations

- Demonstrate that you have read and understood the paper, using concepts and empirical methods discussed in the course.
- Explain the paper's contribution in your own words rather than reproducing its abstract, introduction, or conclusion.
- Distinguish clearly between correlation and causal evidence, and explain why the empirical strategy identifies the effect of interest.
- Think critically about external validity: which features of the original institutional setting matter for applying the results to Lithuania?
- Research the Lithuanian application using credible sources and data.
- Communicate professionally: slides must be clear and readable, the talk must respect the time limit, and arguments must be concise and evidence-based.
- Be prepared to answer questions about the paper, its empirical strategy, its limitations, and the Lithuanian application.
- Engage with the presentations of other groups; ask substantive questions during the sessions.
- Copying text from the assigned paper, another source, another group, or an AI-generated response without appropriate attribution is plagiarism and may result in a grade of zero.

## Use of generative AI

Generative AI tools may be used as supporting tools, but they cannot substitute for reading, understanding, and critically evaluating the paper.

- Every factual statement, empirical result, citation, and source included in the work must be verified by the group.
- AI-generated summaries are not a substitute for reading the assigned paper. During the presentation and discussion, all claims in the submission must be explainable and defensible.
- Invented references, fabricated evidence, or unverified AI-generated claims are treated as serious academic-integrity problems.
- At the end of the written submission, include a short **AI-use statement** indicating whether generative AI was used and, if so, briefly explaining how.
